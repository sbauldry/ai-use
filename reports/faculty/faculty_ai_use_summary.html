AI Use Among Sociology Faculty

Sociology - Summary of the survey of AI use.
Data analysis and report generated with Claude Code (Anthropic), under direction and review

About the survey. An anonymous Qualtrics survey asked faculty whether they used AI tools (LLMs such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, or AI agents such as Claude Code, Codex or Cursor) in their research, teaching and service over the past year, and how often for specific tasks. 26 of 31 faculty responded (84%); 23 wrote open-ended comments. Task questions were shown only to those who used AI in that area, so task results describe users, not all faculty. Read the counts, not just the shares.

Key observations

  • Use varies by area. 18 of 26 respondents used AI in research, 13 in teaching and 6 in service. 5 used it in none of the three areas and 4 in all three. AI agents were used by 4 in research, 3 in teaching and 1 in service.
  • Research use centers on finding, editing and coding. All 18 research users used AI for literature searching (4 regularly); 13 of 18 for editing (9 regularly), 10 for feedback on a draft, 10 for synthesizing literature and 9 for code (7 regularly).
  • Drafting and data analysis are uncommon. 3 of 17 used AI to generate a first draft of text; 3 analyzed quantitative data and 3 qualitative data.
  • Teaching use is mostly for preparation. 10 of 12 teaching users used AI for lecture content, slides or activities and 7 for finding or summarizing readings; 1 used it for grading and 1 for feedback on student work.
  • Service use is limited to 6 people. All 6 used AI for letters of recommendation (1 regularly); other service tasks were used by 3 or fewer.

What faculty say about how they decide

Selected verbatim comments from the open-ended questions (identifying details removed). Themes are the analyst’s reading of the 23 respondents’ comments.

1. AI as a supervised assistant, with judgment kept in human hands

“I direct it and check its work, but I give it specific tasks that I know it is good at and/or that would be tedious and time consuming for me to do. I do not yet feel comfortable -- and may never -- having it produce the ideas or do the writing.”
“I use AI fairly heavily, but never as a fire-and-forget solution. Instead, I generally engage in a dialog with AI, much as I might engage in a dialog with a grad RA.”
“In any case, I check out all responses with follow up questions, including doubts and contradictions.”
“If AI can do it easily or well, I change the assignment if possible.”

2. Where some draw the line: teaching judgment, confidentiality and ethics

“I never use it for grading, feedback, or for writing emails, all of which I feel should come from me directly.”
“it may be unethical to put primary data into AI models (i.e., for reasons of confidentiality).”
“I have questions about the ethical use of AI in qualitative research where assurances of confidentiality is part of the process of receiving IRB approval.”

3. Learning needs and uncertainty about how to use AI well

“I would be interested in a training/workshop specific to understanding how students are using it (as opposed to a workshop how to incorporate it into my teaching).”
“I experimented with a few things but have not found AI effective in doing the tasks I wanted to be done. I need to learn more about various AI tools.”
“I hope to use AI for more labor saving well defined concrete tasks over time … because I haven't yet learned efficient ways to make it work for me.”

Quotes are verbatim from the de-identified data (spelling and punctuation unchanged). Some are excerpts that begin or end mid-sentence.
